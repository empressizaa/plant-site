What Is Plant Spacing?

Plant spacing is the distance between individual plants. Row spacing is the distance between rows.

Plants need enough room for their roots, foliage, sunlight, nutrients and mature growth. Proper spacing can also improve airflow and make watering, pruning and harvesting easier.

Plant Spacing Formula

Rectangular planting:

Estimated plants = Growing area ÷ (plant spacing × row spacing)

Space required:

Space required = Number of plants × (plant spacing × row spacing)

Triangular / hexagonal planting:

Triangular spacing reduces the theoretical area occupied by each plant compared with a rectangular grid, allowing greater planting density when the same spacing is used.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many plants can I fit in my garden?

Enter your plant name and either your garden length and width or your total growing area. The calculator will estimate the number of plants based on the spacing.

What if I only know my garden area?

Use the "I Know My Garden Area" option. You can enter square feet, square meters, acres or hectares.

How much space do I need for 20 plants?

Use the "I Know Number of Plants" option. Enter your plant and the number of plants you want to grow. The calculator will estimate the required growing area.

Can I use this calculator for any plant?

The calculator includes spacing information for many common vegetables, fruits, herbs, flowers, houseplants, succulents, shrubs and trees. Plants that are not in the database can still be calculated by entering their recommended spacing manually.

What if my plant is not in the database?

Enter the recommended plant-to-plant and row-to-row spacing manually. The calculator will then perform the calculation using those values.

Can I calculate plants per acre?

Yes. The calculator converts the spacing into an estimated number of plants per acre.

Can I calculate plants per hectare?

Yes. The calculator also provides an estimated plants-per-hectare figure.

What is triangular plant spacing?

Triangular spacing offsets plants between neighboring rows rather than placing every plant directly beside the plants in the previous row. This can increase theoretical planting density.
